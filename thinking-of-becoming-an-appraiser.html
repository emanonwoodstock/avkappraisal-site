Thinking of Becoming an Appraiser?

Licensing Costs and Requirements

Becoming a Certified Residential appraiser in California typically takes 2 to 3 years and about $2,500 to $5,000 in education, college-level credit, and licensing fees. Experience hours are the main time constraint.

Licensing is overseen by the Bureau of Real Estate Appraisers (BREA), which follows the minimum standards set by the Appraiser Qualifications Board (AQB). Education and experience carry forward from level to level, so nothing is repeated.

Path to Certified Residential
Step Education (cumulative) Experience required Typical time
Appraiser Trainee 75 hours None 2–4 months
Licensed Residential 150 hours 1,000 hours over at least 6 months 6–12 months
Certified Residential 200 hours, plus a college-level education option 1,500 hours over at least 12 months 6–12 months

Experience is cumulative, so the 1,500 hours for Certified Residential include everything logged as a Trainee and Licensed appraiser.

College-level education

A degree isn't strictly required for Certified Residential. BREA accepts any one of the following:

  • A bachelor's degree in any field
  • An associate's degree in business, finance, accounting, economics, or real estate
  • 30 semester hours of specified college courses
  • 30 semester hours earned through College Level Examination Program (CLEP) exams
  • Five years as a Licensed Residential appraiser with no adverse disciplinary history

Estimated licensing costs

Estimated licensing costs
Item Estimated cost

Certified Residential covers residential property of one to four units. Appraising commercial and income property requires the next credential, Certified General, which requires a bachelor's degree, 300 hours of education, and 3,000 hours of experience, including 1,500 hours in non-residential work.

Costs are estimates. Requirements and fees change, so confirm current figures at brea.ca.gov.

Earning Your Experience Hours

The education is the easy part. The hard part is the experience: 1,000 to 1,500 hours that must be earned under a supervising appraiser, with each assignment logged individually.

How it used to work. New appraisers traditionally earned their hours by joining an appraisal firm or working for a lender. Both employed staff appraisers, and a trainee could log a steady flow of assignments while learning on the job.

How it works now. That pathway has narrowed. Today most lenders outsource appraiser selection and management to an appraisal management company (AMC). The few staff appraisers lenders still employ are generally senior review appraisers or commercial appraisers, not people who train newcomers. Most appraisers are now solo practitioners. For them, taking on a trainee means sharing assignments and fees and supervising and signing off on the trainee's work, and many don't find that profitable. That makes supervisors harder to find.

Why the log matters. Your experience log lists every appraisal you count toward your hours, and it is submitted to BREA with your application. BREA will select a few of the appraisals, request the complete workfile for each, and perform an in-depth review. Careful documentation from your first assignment is part of the job.

What this means if you're considering the career:

  • Finding a willing supervising appraiser is usually the first real hurdle and the main factor in how fast you progress.
  • Expect to split your fees with your supervisor. Fee splitting is the norm.
  • Keep a complete, organized workfile for every assignment you log, so it can withstand a BREA review.

Cost of Doing Business

Licensing is only the start. Once you're working on your own, these are the approximate ongoing costs of running a practice in Northern California:

Item Monthly Annual

Multiple Listing Service (MLS) subscription $50 $600

That works out to roughly $750 to $830 per month in fixed costs, or about $18,000 to $20,000 over a two-year renewal cycle. These figures don't include marketing, accounting and tax preparation, office space, or income taxes.

Most appraisers take CE online to control costs. In-person courses run about three times as much but offer deeper instruction and networking. Vehicle costs vary with your assignment mix and how far you drive, so your own mileage may be higher or lower.
